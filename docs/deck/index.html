Reading a rate is the easy part.

A fingertip over the rear camera brightens the picture with every heartbeat. Recovering a number from that is arithmetic. Knowing when the number is wrong is not.

Most of the code in Pulse exists to decide whether the recording can be measured at all. The common correct answer is no.

The knowledge is already in the room. What fails is measurement, and a wrong measurement is worse than none.

The check that does the work

A moving finger reports half the true rate.

The beat detector misses alternate beats, and the answer looks exactly like a slow pulse. Counting beats and reading the frequency spectrum are independent estimates of the same number. Requiring them to agree within 5% is what separates the two.

It refuses 117 recordings that would otherwise have produced a confident, wrong answer.

What is finished, and what is not claimed

Accuracy is limited by length, not by method.

WindowWithin 5 bpm
4 s61%
6 s74%
8 s82%
10 s90%

Every recording in the corpus is ten seconds, which is the ceiling of that table. The app records thirty. That the trend continues is an extrapolation, not a measurement, and the limits page says so.

It diagnoses nothing

It reports a pulse rate and a description of how evenly spaced the beats were, or it reports nothing at all.

Not an atrial-fibrillation screen

The corpus carries no rhythm labels. The irregularity measures are computed and described, never interpreted. Nothing here establishes that they detect anything.

One cohort, a few handsets

Nothing is characterised across skin tones, cold hands, nail polish or camera hardware. PPG is known to behave differently across skin tones.

A refusal is not a finding

It says the recording could not be measured. It says nothing about the person.
